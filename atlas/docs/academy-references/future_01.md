## How they open

Opinion pieces on where AI is going were hard to find in the 4-25 minute window with captions; two runs gave these two IBM explainers, which describe where the field is moving rather than argue a view. The note uses them for how they frame change for a general audience. The seven-terms video opens with two quick truths (AI is everywhere; it changes fast) and a light joke, then invites the viewer to count how many terms they already know. The second opens by naming an unusual new model that does not write text, then borrows a two-speeds-of-thinking idea from a well-known psychology book.

## The order they explain things

- Seven terms: agents (perceive, reason, act, observe in a loop), reasoning models that work step by step, vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, the Model Context Protocol, mixture of experts, then general and super intelligence, each defined then tied to the one before.
- The decision model: fast versus slow thinking, a support email that needs three quick judgments (refund or not, which team, how urgent), why a text model's confidence is unreliable, how this model returns probabilities, then using it as a guardrail beside a language model, and where it could go.

## Examples and analogies

- A toothbrush getting an AI update, as a light opener (IBM).
- Agents in roles: a travel booker, a data analyst, an operations engineer (IBM).
- Quick mental arithmetic versus a long multiplication, for fast and slow thinking (IBM).
- A customer charged twice, routed by three quick decisions (IBM).

## Beginner mistakes they address

- Treating a chatbot and an agent as the same thing (IBM).
- Trusting a model's own statement of how sure it is (IBM).
- Assuming every step of a system needs a large writing model; some steps are quick classifications (IBM).

## Pacing

Measured: about 11 and 15 minutes; both are calm lightboard talks with one drawing per idea, and chapters named by the idea, the searchable pattern. Neither labels any part as opinion, because neither argues one.

## What they do badly, and what we do better

- A list of trends without a single claim the viewer can test; our episode makes one claim (the input matters more than the model choice now) and shows the evidence for it once it is recorded.
- Forecasts of general and super intelligence with no anchor in the viewer's work; we stay with what a small business can try this week.
- Opinion and fact mixed without a label; ours says it is opinion in the scope line and in the description.

## What we take (structure only)

1. Open on the evidence for the claim (the same task on three models, once recorded).
2. State the opinion plainly, labelled as opinion.
3. One idea per drawing, each linked to the last.
4. A light, true example from everyday life, never a forecast with a number.
5. End on one thing the viewer can try, and a question for the comments. This lesson needs the recorded comparison, so it stays held until it exists.
